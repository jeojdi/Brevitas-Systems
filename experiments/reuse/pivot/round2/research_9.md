# Agent Liability: Research Findings

## The headline: the budget line is real, and it was created by insurers withdrawing coverage — not by buyers asking for it

The single most important measured fact in this space is a form filing, not a law. In **January 2026, ISO (Verisk) issued three generative-AI exclusion endorsements for commercial general liability: CG 40 47, CG 40 48, and CG 35 08.** CG 40 47 removes bodily injury, property damage, and personal/advertising injury arising out of generative AI; CG 40 48 narrows to Coverage B (personal and advertising injury) only; CG 35 08 is the third variant. This is corroborated across genuinely independent professional sources — Gallagher, Fenwick, Lexology, Claims Journal, Cloud Security Alliance, and Infosecurity Magazine — so I treat it as **measured fact**, not claim. ISO forms underpin the large majority of US CGL policies, so the "silent AI" era ended in a single form-filing cycle.

This matters more than any legislative development, because it is a *private* forcing function that renews annually and is enforced contractually at every policy renewal. It manufactures a coverage gap by construction, which a broker must then affirmatively fill. That is exactly how a budget line is born.

## Who is liable today: already settled, and it lands on the deployer

There is less legal ambiguity here than the framing "who is responsible?" implies.

- **Case law.** *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149, is the anchor. The tribunal expressly **rejected** Air Canada's argument that the chatbot was a separate agent for whose statements it was not responsible, and found negligent misrepresentation. A deployed agent is not a legal person; its principal wears the loss.
- **Provider terms (verified directly).** Anthropic's Commercial Terms indemnify only third-party **IP** claims arising from authorized use, exclude customer inputs, modifications, and combinations with non-Anthropic tech, cap each party's liability at **fees paid in the previous 12 months**, and exclude consequential damages entirely. There is **no provision addressing agentic or autonomous use, Computer Use, or responsibility for model-initiated actions**. OpenAI's structure is materially the same (IP-scoped indemnity, ~12-month fee cap). A third-party survey of 19 AI agent product terms (15 Aug 2026) reports **zero** indemnities that name agent actions — I flag that one as *claim*, unverified at source, but it is directionally consistent with what I read in the primary terms.
- **Payments.** Visa, Mastercard and Amex have all launched agentic-commerce frameworks, and the schemes have confirmed **existing dispute and chargeback rules apply**. Under current rules the cardholder is accountable for what their agent did, and for goods-and-services disputes **the merchant remains fully liable**.

So the answer to "who is responsible today" is: **the deploying enterprise, with no meaningful recourse upstream.** The model provider has contracted out of it, and the card schemes have declined to invent a new allocation. The gap is not legal uncertainty — it is uninsured, unindemnified, fully-allocated risk sitting on the deployer's balance sheet.

## Is anyone underwriting it? Yes — five funded entrants, a top-three reinsurer, and a hyperscaler, all inside fourteen months

- **AIUC** (Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company): $15M seed, July 2025, led by Nat Friedman. Sells the **AIUC-1** standard (six domains: data & privacy, security, safety, reliability, accountability, societal risk; 5,000+ adversarial simulations; built with Schellman as first accredited auditor, MITRE ATLAS, Orrick, Stanford, CSA). **ElevenLabs went live on the first AIUC-1-backed AI agent policy on 12 Feb 2026.** No limits or premiums disclosed.
- **Armilla**: a **Lloyd's coverholder**, underwritten by certain underwriters at Lloyd's with capacity from Chaucer, AXIS Capital, Convex, Swiss Re and Greenlight Re. Covers third-party financial loss from model underperformance, hallucination, **AI agent mistakes and failure to escalate**, non-breach privacy leakage. Named case study: MKIII, AI loan decisioning.
- **Munich Re**: **HSB AI Liability Insurance launched 18 Mar 2026** for SMBs, explicitly designed to pay AI-related losses "that some General Liability policies exclude" — i.e., a product built to sell into the hole ISO just cut. Plus `aiSure` performance warranties and Cloud Protection+ with Google Cloud.
- **Google Cloud Risk Protection Program**: now offers **affirmative AI insurance coverage** for Google-related AI workloads.
- **Mount** (YC Spring 2026, "The AI Agent Insurance Carrier") and **Klaimee** (YC) — seed-stage, scan/red-team then insure.

Loss experience is thin but non-zero. Gallagher's *Not So Silent* survey found that among respondents who suffered AI-driven losses, **53% were covered in full, 44% partially covered, 3% uninsured** — i.e., losses are already happening and legacy policies are absorbing most of them. Post-CG-40-47, that absorption stops. Separately, AIUC's own arXiv blueprint (Trout et al., July 2026, 31 authors from QBE, Generali, Aon, Stanford, MIT, Anthropic, OpenAI) notes that **~50% of Lloyd's underwriters believe policyholders manage AI risk adequately while only 1 in 5 businesses report mature governance** — a direct statement that the line is currently underpriced. There is **no public paid-claims data**. This line has no loss history.

## Emerging standard of care: converging on private certification, while public regulation retreats

This is the counterintuitive finding, and it cuts against the usual compliance-driven thesis.

**Public regulation is weakening, not strengthening.** The EU **withdrew the AI Liability Directive** (formal withdrawal 16 July 2025, OJ notice 6 Oct 2025), and on **7 May 2026** EU lawmakers agreed to **delay key AI Act compliance deadlines**. In the US, Colorado **repealed and replaced** its landmark AI Act — Governor Polis signed SB26-189 on **14 May 2026**, downgrading to a disclosure-and-rights framework effective 1 Jan 2027 — and the White House has been pushing national preemption of state AI law since a Dec 2025 fact sheet.

**Private standards are filling the vacuum and hardening into procurement gates.** ISO/IEC 42001 is becoming an RFP requirement, particularly in financial services; AIUC-1 is being positioned as "SOC 2 for AI agents"; vendor directories now require SOC 2 Type II plus at least one of ISO 27001 / ISO 42001 / AIUC-1. The standard of care is being set by underwriters and auditors, not legislatures.

## Applying your three rules

**RULE 2 first — size the prize.** The right analogue is cyber. Munich Re puts global cyber GWP at **~$15.3B (2024)**, **~$16.3B expected 2025**, projecting **~$28B by 2030** — a pool that took roughly 25 years to build. AI liability as a distinct line is at effectively **zero today** (first policy Feb 2026). If it tracks cyber, it is a $5–15B GWP pool by the mid-2030s. MGA economics are **8–20% of GWP in commission plus 15–25% of underwriting profit**. So a dominant AI-liability MGA at 10% share of a $10B pool ≈ $1B GWP → roughly **$100–200M revenue**. That is genuinely venture-scale, and it is two to three orders of magnitude better than every number the caching thesis produced (all <1% of a bill). **The prize passes Rule 2 — but only for the entity holding the underwriting pen, not for a vendor selling software into insurers.** That distinction is the whole trap here.

**RULE 1 — what ships free.** Mixed, and it kills the obvious products:
- *Agent observability / audit trail*: **fails**. OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions (v1.37+) are free, Datadog ingests them natively, and CloudTrail is your own cited precedent for hash-chained signed logs shipping free since 2015.
- *Proof an agent was authorized to transact*: **fails**. Google's **AP2** (Sept 2025, open and free) already ships cryptographically-signed Mandates producing a non-repudiable audit trail linking human delegation to agent action.
- *Generic AI insurance wrapper*: **at risk**. Google Cloud already bundles affirmative AI coverage with cloud spend, partnered with Munich Re. This is the hyperscaler-bundling pattern precisely.
- *Independent certification/attestation*: **passes**. A provider cannot certify itself, and the market has already proven it — AIUC needed Schellman, an accredited third party, and Deloitte is building ISO 42001 third-party assurance. **Your structural principle holds, but narrowly: it applies to attestation, not to telemetry.**

The crack worth noting: one source observes that OTel's GenAI conventions "were designed to help engineers debug and monitor systems, not to produce a standardized audit trail suitable for audit." Debug telemetry is not admissible evidence. That distinction is real — but it is a thin moat, because a provider *can* ship a signed action log (AWS did, eleven years ago). What a provider structurally cannot ship is an independent opinion *about itself*.

**RULE 3 — thesis without naming your code.** Passes trivially. "Independent certification and risk transfer for autonomous agent actions" requires no reference to a caching engine. But note the honest inverse: passing Rule 3 this easily means **you have no particular advantage here either.** Rule 3 is necessary, not sufficient, and this domain is a reminder of that.

## The structural read

Insurance is a **risk budget** but it is not a **control point** — it is a capital product, and capital is the part you cannot supply. The control point adjacent to it is the **authorization gate for consequential agent actions**: the inline thing that allows, denies, or transforms a costly action before it executes, and emits the evidentiary record an underwriter prices off and a claims adjuster accepts. That is structurally the Portkey→Palo Alto move — a gateway repositioned as a security control plane — pointed at a risk budget that ISO has just forced open.

But I would not let that conclusion off the hook. The gate is heavily commoditized already (Bedrock Guardrails, MCP approval flows, LangGraph human-in-the-loop interrupts, cloud IAM, AP2 mandates), and AIUC's own eight-component blueprint explicitly consigns incident data collection and ongoing monitoring to "cloud and foundation model provider partnerships" — i.e., the telemetry layer gets absorbed by the platforms, while value accrues to standard-setting, contract design, and pricing. Those are the components an insurer owns, not a software vendor.

## Bottom line

- **"Someone must stand behind agent actions" is becoming a budget line — measured, not speculative.** The forcing function is ISO's CG 40 47 / CG 40 48 / CG 35 08 exclusions effective January 2026, which mechanically create an uninsured gap at every CGL renewal. Corroborated by six independent professional sources.
- **Liability today is already allocated to the deployer, unambiguously.** *Moffatt v. Air Canada* rejected the "the bot is a separate agent" defence; card schemes confirmed existing chargeback rules apply and merchants stay liable; Anthropic and OpenAI indemnify **only IP**, cap liability at ~12 months of fees, and say **nothing** about autonomous actions. There is no upstream recourse.
- **The prize is real and venture-scale — for the risk carrier.** Cyber GWP is ~$15–16B today heading to ~$28B by 2030 (Munich Re); AI liability follows plausibly to $5–15B by the mid-2030s; MGA take is 8–20% of GWP plus 15–25% of profit. A dominant MGA ≈ $100–200M revenue. **This is the first candidate in either round to clear 1% of anything by a wide margin.**
- **But the seat is contested and partly pre-empted.** AIUC ($15M, Nat Friedman, live with ElevenLabs 12 Feb 2026), Armilla (Lloyd's coverholder, Swiss Re/AXIS/Chaucer capacity), Munich Re HSB (launched 18 Mar 2026), Google Cloud (bundling affirmative AI coverage), plus Mount and Klaimee — all inside fourteen months. Hyperscaler bundling is the live Rule 1 threat to the wrapper.
- **RULE 1 kills the software-shaped versions of this.** Agent audit trails ship free (OTel GenAI semconv, Datadog, CloudTrail precedent); agent payment authorization ships free (Google AP2 mandates). Do not build an evidence layer.
- **Your independence principle holds — but only for attestation, not telemetry.** The market pays the accredited auditor (Schellman) and the risk carrier. It does not pay the log vendor. A provider cannot certify itself; it can absolutely ship a signed log.
- **Public regulation is retreating, so do not build a compliance-deadline thesis.** EU AI Liability Directive withdrawn (Oct 2025); EU AI Act deadlines delayed (7 May 2026); Colorado AI Act repealed and replaced (14 May 2026); federal preemption being pushed. The standard of care is being set privately by underwriters, ISO 42001 and AIUC-1 — which is slower but more durable.
- **The line has no loss history and is admittedly underpriced.** No public paid-claims data; ~50% of Lloyd's underwriters think policyholders manage AI risk adequately while only 1 in 5 firms have mature governance. Anyone entering as a carrier is pricing blind, and the first bad accumulation event will reprice the market.
- **Honest caveat on sources.** The ISO exclusion fact, provider terms, *Moffatt*, cyber GWP, Munich Re/Armilla/AIUC products and the Gallagher survey are solid. A large share of the search surface on this topic (zylos.ai, flowverify.co, gridex.dev, getcaic.org, agentinsured.eu, vettedaiagents.com, underwriting-agents.com) is low-quality or apparently AI-generated SEO content, and market-size figures from research mills ranged incoherently from $0.8B to $7B for the same year — I discarded those and sized bottoms-up from cyber instead.
- **If you pursue this, the seat to want is the authorization gate, not the policy.** Inline, allow/deny/transform on consequential agent actions, feeding an underwriter — a control point in a risk budget. But test Rule 1 hard first: Bedrock Guardrails, MCP approval flows, LangGraph interrupts, IAM and AP2 already occupy much of that ground for free.
