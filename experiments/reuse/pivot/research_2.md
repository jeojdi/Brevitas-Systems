## What actually happened, case by case

**CDN caching vs. cloud-native edge.** This is the cleanest instance of the pattern and it has a corpse. Limelight Networks IPO'd in 2007 raising $240M, bought Edgecast from Yahoo for ~$300M in June 2022 and rebranded as Edgio, filed Chapter 11 on 9 Sept 2024, sold its assets to Lynrock Lake for ~$110M — Akamai separately bought the customer contracts and patents — and was formally defunct 15 Jan 2025 (measured fact, per public filings summarized on Wikipedia). Limelight never moved off the commoditized unit: it sold delivered bytes, priced per GB, to the same buyer, while AWS/Cloudflare/Google drove that price toward zero.

Akamai is the survivor and its financials show exactly what "moved sideways/up" looks like in numbers. Q2 2026: Security $604M (+10% YoY), Cloud Infrastructure Services $99M (+39%), and Delivery-and-other $396M (**−6%**). A year earlier, Q2 2025: Security $552M (+11%), Delivery $320M (−3%), Cloud Computing $171M (+13%) of which CIS was $71M (+30%). Note the segment reclassification between those two prints — Akamai folded most of "Cloud Computing" into "Delivery and Other Cloud Applications" and now reports Linode-derived CIS separately — so the two tables are not directly subtractable, but the direction is unambiguous and measured: the original caching business is in permanent single-digit decline and is no longer the largest segment; security is, at roughly half of a ~$4.2B company. Akamai's claim that it signed >$2.8B of multi-year CIS contracts year-to-date including one >$600M/4yr is a vendor claim.

Fastly is the ambiguous middle: $624.0M revenue in 2025 against a $121.7M net loss, an 11% layoff in Aug 2024, $150M of convertible notes at a 7.75% yield in Dec 2024 (a distressed coupon), a CEO change in June 2025, and a move off the NYSE to Nasdaq in Dec 2025 (measured facts). Its homepage today still leads "Build, Secure, and Deliver" with no AI product line. My inference: Fastly attempted the same up-and-sideways move as Akamai from a much weaker balance sheet and has not yet cleared it.

**APM/monitoring vs. cloud-provider-native monitoring.** The outcomes here are more instructive than the folklore. AppDynamics sold to Cisco for $3.7B in Jan 2017, days before its IPO; it was merged into Splunk and became formally defunct in 2025 — the brand did not survive absorption twice over. Splunk itself, $3.65B revenue in 2023, sold to Cisco for $28B (announced 21 Sept 2023, closed 18 Mar 2024). New Relic, $926M revenue, went private to Francisco Partners and TPG for $6.5B (announced July 2023, closed Nov 2023). Sumo Logic went private to Francisco Partners for $1.7B in May 2023. That is four of the five 2010s-era monitoring franchises exiting to strategics or PE inside 24 months.

The two that did not: Datadog, $3.43B revenue in 2025 and added to the S&P 500 in July 2025, and Grafana Labs, which raised $270M at a $6B valuation in 2024 after $220M at $3B in 2021. My inference on why: neither was actually absorbed. CloudWatch and Azure Monitor took the floor of the market — the "is it up" tier — but never took the cross-cloud, cross-signal correlation layer. Datadog escaped by module proliferation (each new module is a new budget line in the same account); Grafana escaped by owning the open standard (AGPLv3 in 2021, then buying k6, Pyroscope, Asserts.ai, TailCtrl, LogLine) so that the platform's own telemetry flows *through* it. That is a move UP to correlation and insight, not sideways.

The genuinely new entrants both moved to a *different buyer with a different unit of value*. Chronosphere — $11M Series A 2019, $200M at $1B+ in Oct 2021, $115M at $1.6B in Jan 2023, bought Calyptia in Jan 2024 — repositioned from "cloud-native monitoring" to "stop paying for data you don't use," claiming 89% average reduction in low-value data volumes (vendor claim), and was acquired by Palo Alto Networks for **$3.35B** (announced Nov 2025, completed Jan 2026). Cribl runs the same play — "The AI Platform for Telemetry," data reduction and vendor independence — and is notable for having been sued by Splunk in April 2024 and losing on liability for exactly **$1** in damages, which tells you how little of the incumbent's economic value the pipeline layer actually took. Both companies sell the *reduction* of the incumbent's bill, and both sell increasingly to the SOC rather than to SRE.

**Redis/Memcached-as-a-service vs. managed platform caches.** ElastiCache and its peers absorbed "run Redis for me" almost completely. Redis Ltd's response was three moves, all measurable: it changed the license (BSD → RSAL/SSPL in March 2024, provoking the Linux Foundation's Valkey fork, then adding AGPL in v8.0 in May 2025 after the fork had made the restriction unenforceable in practice); it bought a stream-processing company, Decodable, in Sept 2025; and it repositioned the entire product line around agent context — LangCache, Agent Memory, Context Retriever, and "Redis Iris" launched 18 May 2026 under the banner "Context is all you need." LangCache's marketing claims "Save 90% on API costs" and cites a customer at a 70% semantic-cache hit rate; both are vendor claims and, given your own cold-share measurement, I would treat the 70% figure as reflecting a chatbot FAQ workload, not agent traffic. Memcached-as-a-service produced no surviving independent vendor that I could verify.

**Build caching vs. free CI caches.** The most on-the-nose case in your entire comparison set. Gradle Technologies **renamed itself Develocity on 30 June 2026** and moved to develocity.ai; gradle.com now 301s to it. Its stated reason: "keeping software delivery governed and fast as AI moves the bottleneck from writing code to reviewing it." Its headline is "Give AI the context to deliver software, with **Governance** and Efficiency," and its product list is now split into a Governance half (Build Scan, Policy Scan, Artifact Governance, On-Demand Auditing) and an Efficiency half (Build Cache, Test Distribution, Predictive Test Selection, Failure Analytics, Flaky Test Detection). A build-cache company put governance *first* on the page and renamed the company after the observability product, not the cache. That is a documented move UP, and it took roughly a decade.

The others diverge. BuildBuddy stayed technical and went *down* into compute — remote build execution, "Enterprise Bazel." Critically for you: on 5 Jan 2026 it shipped **Action Merging**, which is precisely single-flight deduplication of concurrent identical in-flight actions, for the exact reason you found — "Bazel's caching only works for completed actions." Their reported production numbers are >1,000 merged actions/second at peak, hundreds of invocations merging against one primary execution, and nearly 3 hours saved on some long-running tests (vendor claim, but operationally specific). Nx Cloud repositioned from caching to cost-per-CI-run and then to agents: Self-Healing CI (June 2025), "Nx Agents now 4x faster & 30% cheaper than GitHub Actions" (June 2026), Polygraph (June 2026), and blog framing of monorepos as "infrastructure for AI agents." Depot went furthest sideways — from Docker build acceleration into being a platform: $10M Series A in March 2026 (Felicis, YC, Pioneer Fund), Depot CI GA (March 2026), Registry v2 (April), Depot Metal compute (July), Depot Code, a diskless git server (July), under the line "Code isn't the bottleneck anymore. Your build pipeline is."

The pattern across all four: **none of them still sells cache hits.** They sell seats and governance (Develocity), compute minutes (Depot, BuildBuddy, Nx Agents), or agent-loop reliability. Caching became the wedge and the demo, never the meter.

**Database query caches.** The purest test, and the answer is stark. PolyScale.ai — the "global edge cache for your database, no code changes" company — is gone; **polyscale.ai now redirects to a BrandBucket listing offering the domain for $8,195** (measured, checked today). ReadySet is still live but has repositioned to "databases weren't built for AI," selling 30–70% infrastructure cost reduction and protection against unpredictable AI-generated SQL (vendor claims). No independent query-cache vendor of scale exists. My inference: query caching is the closest structural analogue to your reuse layer — transparent, correctness-risky, and worth a percentage of a bill the platform is already discounting — and it produced zero durable businesses.

**CI/CD vendors vs. GitHub Actions.** Travis CI, acquired by Idera in Jan 2019, killed its free OSS tier in Nov 2020 and effectively ceded the open-source market that had been its distribution. CircleCI raised $315M at a $1.7B valuation in 2021, cut 17% of staff in Dec 2022, and suffered a credential-exposing breach in Jan 2023; it persists but I found no evidence of a valuation event since. Harness is the one that clearly moved: a $240M Series E, an acquisition of Qwiet AI for application security, and a homepage that now reads "**AI for Everything After Code**" spanning CI/CD, feature management, security testing, cloud *and AI* cost management, and an internal developer portal, with Autonomous Worker Agents (June 2026) and an MCP Gateway (Aug 2026). Harness did not out-CI GitHub; it wrapped GitHub in governance and sold the wrapper to enterprises.

**The one case that is your case.** Portkey — an LLM gateway selling exactly what you would be selling: unified access to 1,600+ models, observability, guardrails, prompt management, cost attribution, and *intelligent caching* — was acquired by Palo Alto Networks (listed April 2026) and its homepage banner now reads "**Portkey is now PRISMA AIRS AI Gateway**." The AI-gateway category's first real exit went to a security company, and what got bought was the governance surface: RBAC, cost attribution, audit logging, PII redaction. Not the cache. Meanwhile Cloudflare's AI Gateway — which includes response caching — is documented as "available on all plans," i.e. the platform is already giving the caching layer away, exactly as it gave away CDN caching and CloudWatch.

## Up or sideways?

Both, in a specific sequence, and the sequence matters. Every survivor I can verify moved **UP first** — from the commoditized mechanism to governance, insight, or workflow over the same data — and then, at exit, moved **SIDEWAYS to the security budget**. Chronosphere ($3.35B), Portkey, Splunk ($28B), AppDynamics-into-Splunk, Cribl's SOC pivot, Harness buying Qwiet, Develocity leading with audit and policy. The security buyer is the one who pays for *provenance and control* rather than for *savings*, and savings is precisely the claim the platform can undercut to zero at will.

The ones that moved sideways *without* moving up — same buyer, same unit, better implementation — are the dead ones: Edgio, Travis CI, PolyScale.

On leading indicators, this is my inference from the case set rather than measured, but it is consistent across all of them. The vendors that made it showed, *before* the platform's absorption bit into revenue: (1) a **second buyer already on the invoice** — compliance, security, finance — not merely named in a deck; (2) a **pricing unit decoupled from the commoditized mechanism** (seats, data volume reduced, compute minutes) rather than cache hits or GB delivered; and (3) **retained possession of a data exhaust the platform structurally cannot reconstruct** — Develocity's build scans, Grafana's cross-vendor telemetry position, Chronosphere's control plane over what data ever gets stored. Indicator (3) is the one that maps to your "byproduct nobody costed": a complete, content-addressed operation graph per run with metered tokens and upstream edges is the same class of asset as a build scan, and Develocity is the existence proof that the scan outlived the cache as the company's identity — its August 2026 post claims an agent given real build data succeeded in 36 of 40 runs versus 4 of 40 without (vendor claim, unreplicated, and the setup is not disclosed on the index page).

The counter-indicator is equally consistent: a vendor whose headline number is a percentage of someone else's bill is negotiating against a platform that can move that percentage to zero unilaterally, and did — OpenAI's 24h retention at no premium, Anthropic's read-refreshing 5-minute tier, Cloudflare's free gateway cache.

## Bottom line

**Established (measured, from primary or public filings):**
- Pure caching-as-a-product produced no durable standalone business in any of the five domains examined. PolyScale's domain is for sale for $8,195; Edgio is defunct; Memcached-as-a-service left no independent survivor; no build-cache vendor still meters cache hits.
- The survivors all repositioned off the cache: Gradle Technologies renamed itself **Develocity** on 30 June 2026 and leads with Governance; Redis relicensed twice and rebuilt its line around agent context (LangCache, Agent Memory, Iris, May 2026); Akamai's delivery segment is at −6% YoY while Security is $604M/quarter; Depot, Nx Cloud and BuildBuddy sell compute and agent-loop reliability.
- The terminal exits went to **security buyers**: Chronosphere → Palo Alto Networks for $3.35B (Nov 2025 announced / Jan 2026 closed); Portkey → Palo Alto Networks, now Prisma AIRS AI Gateway; Splunk → Cisco $28B; AppDynamics defunct 2025 inside Splunk. New Relic ($6.5B) and Sumo Logic ($1.7B) went to PE, not to growth.
- **Concurrent-execution deduplication is a proven, shipped mechanism in the build world**: BuildBuddy's Action Merging, GA Jan 2026, reportedly merging >1,000 actions/sec in production. Your fan-out finding has a working precedent — and that precedent shipped as a *feature of a remote-execution platform*, not as a company.
- The platform is already absorbing the AI-gateway caching layer: Cloudflare AI Gateway includes response caching on all plans.

**Contested (defensible either way on current evidence):**
- Whether Datadog and Grafana are "survivors of absorption" at all. My reading is that CloudWatch/Azure Monitor absorbed the *floor* and never contested correlation, which would make the APM analogy weaker support for your case than it first appears — the platform there stopped short, whereas in caching it went all the way.
- Whether build-cache vendors survived *because* they moved up or merely because caching was always a loss-leader for compute. None of Depot, BuildBuddy, Nx or Develocity discloses a revenue mix, so the causal claim is unfalsifiable from outside.
- Whether Portkey's outcome demonstrates that AI governance is a real buyer, or is an artifact of Palo Alto's 2025–26 acquisition spree (Protect AI $500M, CyberArk $25B, Koi ~$400M, Chronosphere $3.35B, Portkey). One data point inside a shopping binge.
- Whether "up" and "sideways" are separable at all. In every case I verified they happened together, which means the case set cannot adjudicate the question you posed as an either/or.

**Unknown (I could not verify; treat as open):**
- Revenue, ARR or growth for every private vendor in the comparison — Develocity, BuildBuddy, Nx/Nrwl, Depot, Cribl, ReadySet, Redis Ltd. Redis's post-relicense revenue in particular is unpublished, so whether the context/agent-memory pivot is *working* is genuinely unknown, not merely uncertain.
- Whether any vendor has successfully monetized a build/operation graph as a standalone product rather than as an attach to acceleration. Develocity's Build Scan is bundled, not separately priced, as far as I can determine.
- Fastly's current segment mix, and therefore whether its up-and-sideways attempt is working.
- Time-to-recovery. I could not establish how long any of these transitions took from "platform absorbed it" to "new segment exceeds old." Akamai's took roughly a decade; Develocity's took about eleven years from Gradle Enterprise. If that is the characteristic timescale, it is a materially different fact for a funded incumbent than for you.
- Whether the security-buyer pattern is causal or an accident of who happened to have M&A capital in 2023–2026.
